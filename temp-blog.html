Gausyn v0.1

Ninety-nine commits, one whiteboard, and a tutor that learned to teach a lesson, hand over a worksheet, and keep its hands off your answers. Here is everything in the first release, and what it taught us.

v0.1 May 20 – July 27, 2026 · 99 commits · in private testing

How it got built.

We did not start from a model and look for a use. We started from a whiteboard, because that is where tutoring actually happens — two people, one surface, a pen. Everything since has been an answer to a question a real session raised: how does it know where to write? what happens when the student interrupts? where do the worksheets come from? who decides the lesson is over?

Release notes

What is actually in v0.1.

Written for the people who want the specifics: what works today, how it works, and where the edges still are.

The whiteboard

The session is a real canvas, not a chat window with a picture in it. Pen, eraser, shapes, a select tool for moving things around, and multiple board tabs per session. Upload a PDF worksheet and it renders page by page for you to write straight on top of; upload a photo of homework and it is scaled and centred for you. Boards can be plain, lined, or graph paper.

Every board tab independently remembers its strokes, its PDF, its scroll position, its background, and every hint Gausyn has placed on it. Switch to another tab and back and nothing has moved — which sounds small until you have lost twenty minutes of working out because a tool decided to tidy up after you.

The tutor loop

Every exchange, Gausyn captures what is actually on your board and sends it along with what you said. It replies with speech and, when useful, with something written on the board — a plain-words nudge, a single typeset step of math, or a generated diagram. It is Socratic by default: the system prompt is built to withhold the answer and ask the question that unlocks it.

The part we are proudest of is the least visible. When a worksheet loads, Gausyn solves every numbered problem to a full reference solution before the student writes anything, and grounds the rest of the session against it. That is why it can tell you your third line is right rather than agreeing pleasantly with whatever you wrote.

Where hints stand

An AI that picks pixel coordinates will eventually write over your work. So Gausyn does not pick them. When a typed PDF loads, we read the page’s own text geometry — where each question line starts, where the underscore blanks are, where the multiple-choice options sit — and hints are placed by a deterministic engine against that map. Fill-in-the-blank gets a nudge beside the blank; multiple choice gets one near the options; show-your-work gets a step where there is vertical room, and a suggestion to open a workspace where there is not.

Your ink, the worksheet’s own text, and shapes all block placement. When the column fills up, the oldest hints are pruned rather than stacked into a pile. Scanned worksheets fall back to a vision pass, which is the weakest link in the chain today.

Teacher, Practice, and Homework

A session opens in one of three shapes. Teacher mode pulls a lesson matched to the session’s subject, grade, and topic and walks it page by page, out loud. During a walkthrough the toolbar is hidden and Gausyn is deliberately speech-only — it will not write on the board, because the lesson is the thing to follow. Interrupt with the mic or a typed line and the walkthrough pauses; say “continue lesson” and it resumes from the page it stopped on.

Practice mode takes the paired worksheet written to match that lesson and unlocks the full toolset. Homework mode skips the bank entirely and expects your own upload. You can move between them mid-session by saying so — “let’s practise”, “switch to teacher mode”, “switch to homework”, or “get me a worksheet on trig identities” — without rejoining and without losing the conversation.

The worksheet bank

Behind the modes is a bank of lesson and practice PDFs, tagged with subject, grade levels, and topic keywords, stored privately and matched by score when a session starts. Lessons carry a pointer to the practice sheet written to go with them, which is what makes the handoff feel like one continuous class rather than two apps.

The honest limitation of v0.1: the bank is small, and seeded by us. Filling it — with teachers’ own material, at every grade and in every subject — is the single biggest piece of work in front of the product.

Ask Gausyn workspaces

Ask Gausyn is the answer to the oldest problem in worksheets: the margin is two inches and the thinking is two pages. Drag a box around a problem and it becomes its own tab — the crop pinned at the top as the reference, a clean grid underneath, and an anchor pin left on the source board so you can jump straight back. Hints follow the same rules inside, stacking below the pinned reference.

Drag-to-cropPinned referenceQ1, Q2, Q3 tabsAnchor pin home

Generated practice

Ask for more practice on any topic in the session’s subject and Gausyn writes a question itself, one at a time, into a dedicated Practice tab pinned at the top of the board where it cannot be scrolled away or accidentally closed. Say “harder”, “easier”, “a different one”, or “next problem” and the tab is cleared and rewritten — even mid-problem, if you have decided you picked the wrong fight.

Voice, and who is speaking

Voice in, voice out. The microphone only listens when you click it, except in Freeform mode, where Gausyn listens after it finishes speaking and submits when you stop — with noise gating so a passing bus does not become a question. Text chat is an equal alternative at every step, for the library and the late-night session in a shared room.

v0.1 also gave the tutor an identity: named personas mapped to subjects, each with its own voice, levelled so nobody arrives shouting, and a greeting that uses the student’s name and their stated learning style.

The dashboard that plans the week

Onboarding takes five steps — name and grade, subjects, goals and learning style, any files you want to hand over, and a drag-to-select weekly availability grid — and ends with a four-week lesson plan generated onto the calendar.

After that the calendar is conversational. Tell it you have a chemistry exam next Friday and prep sessions appear inside your real availability; tell it Wednesdays are bad and it re-plans. Deadlines are tracked separately from lessons, with a countdown. A daily check-in asks what changed and lets you attach a photo of tonight’s homework — we read it once for a summary, use that to shape the session, and delete the file rather than keep it.

Sessions are real class periods. Joining opens ten minutes early, the countdown is anchored on the server rather than in your browser, and when time is up the tutor itself stops answering — you cannot extend a lesson by editing a URL or reloading the page. The calendar then records what actually happened: completed, how long, how much was covered.

What is not in v0.1

Projects and Library are visible in the navigation but not built yet, and the Learning tab has full unit breakdowns for high-school math while other subjects still show topic grids. Scanned and handwritten worksheets work through a vision fallback that is noticeably weaker than the typed-PDF path. The bank needs to be an order of magnitude larger. And every hour of tutoring costs real money to run, which is why sessions carry a usage budget today — getting that cost down is a product goal, not just an infrastructure one, because a personal teacher that only some families can afford is the problem we started with.

What v0.2 is about.
